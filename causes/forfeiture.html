Cause

Civil asset forfeiture

Police can take your cash or car without charging you with a crime, and you have to sue to get it back. Read that twice.

Say it in 10 seconds: "Police shouldn't keep your money when nobody was charged with a crime."

A control exposure message, in the language of the messaging playbook.

Where things stand

  • Since 2000, civil and criminal forfeiture has generated at least $82 billion nationwide, according to the Institute for Justice's Policing for Profit (4th edition). As of 2026-03. Source: Institute for Justice (opens in a new tab)
  • In states with usable data, nearly half of forfeitures happen without anyone, let alone the owner, being convicted of a crime. As of 2026-03. Source: Institute for Justice (opens in a new tab)
  • Fighting a forfeiture rarely pays: IJ estimates a simple state-court case costs about $3,300, nearly twice the median cash forfeiture of $1,678 across 24 states. Most cases end by default. As of 2026-03. Source: Institute for Justice (opens in a new tab)
  • Maine became the fourth state to end civil forfeiture in 2021, joining New Mexico (2015), Nebraska (2016) and North Carolina. Those states require a criminal case before property can be forfeited. As of 2021. Source: Institute for Justice (opens in a new tab)
  • Thirty-five states and the federal government get an overall grade of D+ or below for their forfeiture laws, and Massachusetts gets the only F. As of 2025 grades. Source: Institute for Justice (opens in a new tab)

Do this locally

  1. Request your county's and police department's forfeiture records and federal equitable-sharing reports, and publish what you find.
  2. Testify for state bills that require a criminal conviction before forfeiture and send proceeds to the general fund instead of police budgets.
  3. Ask your city council to limit local police participation in federal equitable sharing.

Each one is a real-world win you can show voters. That's the whole Asymmetric Libertarian idea: pick one system, fix it, make it visible.

Who's already fighting

  • Institute for Justice (opens in a new tab): Public-interest law firm that publishes Policing for Profit, grades state forfeiture laws and represents property owners.
  • Cato Institute (opens in a new tab): Libertarian think tank in Washington, D.C., that publishes research on criminal justice and civil liberties, including forfeiture.

Listing an organization isn't an endorsement or a partnership. It's who's doing the work.
