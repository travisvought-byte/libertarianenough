Step 4: Cap it and set a clock

Every win can be undone a little at a time. Spending caps and expiration dates make government ask permission to grow, instead of you asking permission to live.

Why it comes here

Government rarely grows in one big vote. The book: "Complexity rarely arrives all at once. It accumulates." So the fix has to be automatic too. A spending cap stops the state from quietly spending every new dollar. A sunset clause means a law or agency ends on a set date unless lawmakers vote to keep it, so the burden of proof flips.

These come fourth because they protect what steps 1 to 3 won, and because voters accept limits more easily once they've seen less government work.

"Remove rules as aggressively as they are added."

The Asymmetric Libertarian, chapter 4

Proof it works

  • In 1992, Colorado voters approved the Taxpayer's Bill of Rights, 53.7% to 46.3%. It caps what the state can collect and spend, requires voter approval for new taxes, and refunds money collected above the cap. Source: Ballotpedia (opens in a new tab)
  • In fiscal year 2024 alone, Colorado owed taxpayers about $1.7 billion in refunds under that cap. Source: 9News (opens in a new tab)
  • Since 1977, Texas's sunset process has abolished 95 state agencies, 42 of them completely. Agencies under review end automatically unless lawmakers vote to keep them. Reviews from 1985 to 2023 produced about $1 billion in savings and revenue. Source: Texas Sunset Advisory Commission (opens in a new tab)
  • Colorado, Texas and Alabama were among the first states to adopt sunset reviews, in the 1970s. By the 1980s, 36 states had them. Source: NCSL (opens in a new tab)

What it looks like where you live

In your town

  • Ask for a sunset on every new city board, fee and program: it ends in five years unless the council votes to keep it.
  • Read your city or county budget. Find the three lines that grew fastest over five years and ask why at the next meeting.
  • When a levy or tax comes up for a vote, ask when it ends. If the answer is never, that's your argument.

Your state party

  • Push a sunset review law if your state doesn't have one, or put more agencies under it if it does. Texas is the model.
  • In states where citizens can put measures on the ballot, take a spending limit straight to voters. Colorado's passed with 53.7%.
  • Ask every candidate one question: name one state program you'd let expire.

You, this week

  • Go to one budget hearing. Sit in the front. Ask one question.
  • Look up how much your state's budget grew in the last ten years, and share that one number with three people who complain about taxes.
  • When anyone proposes a new program, ask them in public when it ends.

Say it in 10 seconds: "Every new program gets an expiration date. If it works, lawmakers can vote to keep it. If it doesn't, it ends on its own."

Built on the book's pattern: what the system does, why it hurts you, what you get when it's gone. More in messages that land.

Watch out for

Caps with an escape hatch. A limit lawmakers can waive with a simple majority is a suggestion. Look for limits that take a vote of the people, or a supermajority, to break.
