The plan: the debate

Push the button, or take the loaf?

Libertarians agree on less government. They fight about how fast, how far, and whether politics is even the way to get there. Here's every side, fairly, in their own words.

The button: end it now

In April 1946, Leonard Read gave a talk in Detroit called "I'd Push the Button," a month after he founded the Foundation for Economic Education. He opened with a picture: "If there were a button on this rostrum, the pressing of which would release all wage and price controls instantaneously," he said, "I would put my finger on it and push!" Sources: Foundation for Economic Education (opens in a new tab), Foundation for Economic Education (opens in a new tab)

In 1977, Murray Rothbard made the button the test of a real abolitionist: someone who "would blister his thumb pushing a button that would abolish the State immediately, if such a button existed." You'll often see it quoted as "blister my thumb." His actual words were "his thumb." Source: Mises Institute (opens in a new tab)

Their case: if the state is wrong, it's wrong today. Going slow means more people jailed, taxed and pushed around while you wait. And people who get comfortable with small steps tend to stop pushing.

The loaf: take what you can get, never lose the goal

Even Rothbard didn't sit and wait for a button. In the same essay, he said the abolitionist "will take a bit of the loaf if necessary—while always preferring the whole loaf if he can achieve it." Source: Mises Institute (opens in a new tab)

In his book For a New Liberty, he set the rule for which partial steps count: "A short-run demand may not go as far as we would like, but it should always be consistent with the final end." Source: Reason (opens in a new tab)

A 2006 Mises Institute essay by Albert Esplugas put the middle ground plainly: "Where it is not possible to take giant leaps it is not condemnable but preferable to take small ones." Source: Mises Institute (opens in a new tab)

The small state: stop at the night watchman

Robert Nozick's 1974 book Anarchy, State, and Utopia argued for a minimal state, one that protects people against violence, theft and fraud, and not for no state at all. Source: Britannica (opens in a new tab)

Writer Sheldon Richman argued that limited reforms, like the trucking deregulation of the 1970s, were worth doing even though they fell far short of libertarian goals. Source: Reason (opens in a new tab)

Their case: every step that works earns trust for the next one. You can't sell a stateless society to people who have never seen a permit office that works. And a small state is a real place to stop, not just a rest area.

Around the state: the agorists

In his New Libertarian Manifesto (1980), Samuel Edward Konkin III called for counter-economics, living around the state instead of working through it. He mocked the effort to win liberty through political parties as "Partyarchy." "Agorism" comes from agora, the Greek word for an open marketplace. In his words: "The actual practice of human actions that evade, avoid, and defy the State is counter-economic activity." Source: The Anarchist Library (opens in a new tab)

Their case: you don't need a vote to live free. Every private deal, homeschooled kid and neighbor-to-neighbor trade shrinks the state's reach today.

The catch: going around the state can mean breaking laws, and the people who get caught pay the price. This site doesn't tell anyone to do that. The legal parts of the agorist toolkit, like co-ops, private arbitration and mutual aid, are exactly what step 6 needs.

The movers: pick one place and win it

The Free State Project started in 2001 with a goal of getting 20,000 liberty-minded people to move to one small state. It announced 20,000 pledges to move to New Hampshire on February 3, 2016, and about 2,000 members had already moved by then. Sources: Free State Project (opens in a new tab), Fox News (opens in a new tab)

Their case: spread thin, libertarians lose everywhere. Packed into one place, they can win local seats and show the rest of the country what works. That's step 5 with a moving truck.

The "you do you" crowd

Plenty of people agree with libertarians on a lot and still won't spend a Saturday on it. The numbers show the gap. In an October 2025 Gallup poll, 64% of U.S. adults said marijuana should be legal. In May 2025, 68% supported same-sex marriage. But in Gallup's March 2025 poll, only 1% named drugs, and 1% named LGBTQ rights, as the country's most important problem. Sources: Gallup (opens in a new tab), Gallup (opens in a new tab), Gallup (opens in a new tab)

Even sex work, the cause most politicians won't touch, had 52% support for decriminalizing it and 35% against in a November 2019 poll by Data for Progress, a progressive think tank. It's the newest national poll we found. Source: Reason (opens in a new tab)

Their case: live and let live is the whole point. Not everyone has to be an activist, and freedom includes the freedom not to care about politics.

How to work with them: don't ask them to fight. Ask them to sign one petition, cast one vote, or just not stand in the way. The book splits any coalition into rings: the committed core, people who agree on outcomes, and an outer ring who mostly want relief. Its rule is "You win by expanding outward— not arguing inward."

One test everybody can use

Rothbard's rule and the book's rule land in the same place. Before you back a reform, ask one question: does this take force out of someone's life, or does it just move the force to a different office?

  • If it takes force out, it's on the road, whatever you think the end looks like. Back it.
  • If it adds a new agency, fee or gatekeeper, it's a detour, however libertarian it sounds.
