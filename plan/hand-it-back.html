Last, hand services back to people who'll run them voluntarily. This step comes last on purpose: it only works when the replacement is already standing.

Why it comes here

This is the step people picture when they hear "libertarian," and it's the one most likely to fail if you rush it. The book's warning fits here better than anywhere: take rules away without redesigning the system, and you get new gatekeepers instead of freedom.

So build first. Grow the volunteer, charity and private options until the government version isn't needed, then let it go. Test every handoff, measure it, publish the results and be ready to change course. This is the least tested step on the road, which is exactly why it comes after the tested ones.

"Remove constraints without redesigning the system, and you amplify asymmetry, selection pressure, and new gatekeepers—not autonomy."

The Asymmetric Libertarian, chapter 2

Proof it works

  • Volunteers make up 62% of U.S. firefighters, and 18,873 of the nation's 29,452 fire departments are all volunteer. Their donated time saves local governments an estimated $46.9 billion a year. Source: National Volunteer Fire Council (opens in a new tab)
  • In 2005, Sandy Springs, Georgia, started running most city services through private contractors. Around 2010, splitting the work among several firms was expected to save about $7 million a year. In 2019 the city moved to a mix of contractors and city staff, and it reports savings from bringing some services back in-house too. Treat it as an experiment with a lesson: test, measure and keep what works. Source: City of Sandy Springs (opens in a new tab)
  • Steps 2 and 3 already handed some things back. Seven states let neighbors sell most homemade food without a state inspection, and 18 states let families take education money to the school they choose. Sources: Institute for Justice (opens in a new tab), Ballotpedia (opens in a new tab)

What it looks like where you live

In your town

  • Join or give to your volunteer fire department, food bank or mutual aid group. They're the replacement, and they need people now.
  • Ask your city which services it already contracts out, what it pays, and how that compares with doing it in-house.
  • Start something small that government does badly: a neighborhood cleanup, a tool library, rides to appointments for seniors.

Your state party

  • Run a pilot, not a purge. Pick one service, hand it to a contractor or nonprofit for two years with clear measures, and publish the results either way.
  • Look for state rules that block volunteers, like permits for charity events or limits on who can give rides or share food, and work to remove them.
  • Celebrate the people already doing it. Name the best volunteer group in each county every year.

You, this week

  • Volunteer two hours with a group that does something government also does.
  • Ask that group which rule or permit slows them down most, and bring the answer to your state party.
  • Pay a neighbor for something they do well instead of waiting for a program.

Say it in 10 seconds: "Most fire departments in America already run on volunteers. People take care of each other when government gets out of the way."

Built on the book's pattern: what the system does, why it hurts you, what you get when it's gone. More in messages that land.

Watch out for

Pulling the rug. Ending a service before the replacement can carry the load hurts the people who depend on it, and it hands the other side its best argument.
