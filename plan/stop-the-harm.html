Start by ending the punishments that wreck lives over peaceful choices. These reforms have the widest support and the clearest wins.

Why it comes here

This is where government does the most damage to the most people, and where the most voters already agree with us. A cannabis charge or a seized car can cost someone their job, their savings and their kids. The book judges a system by what happens when someone fails, and it puts it simply: "People will not take risks they cannot survive." A criminal record is a failure that never ends.

It comes first because it hurts the most, and because winning it shows people that less government can mean more safety, not less. It also fixes bad incentives. When police keep what they seize, the system rewards taking. Require a conviction and that reward goes away.

"One mistake should not define a lifetime."

The Asymmetric Libertarian, chapter 5

Proof it works

  • In 2012, Colorado and Washington became the first states to legalize marijuana for adults. As of 2026, 24 states and D.C. allow it, and 53% of Americans live where it's legal. Source: Pew Research Center (opens in a new tab)
  • Ohio voters passed Issue 2 in November 2023 with 57.19% of the vote, making Ohio the 24th state to legalize recreational marijuana. Source: Ballotpedia (opens in a new tab)
  • In an October 2025 Gallup poll, 64% of U.S. adults said marijuana should be legal, including 40% of Republicans. Source: Gallup (opens in a new tab)
  • In 2015, New Mexico's legislature voted unanimously to abolish civil forfeiture. The state can now take property only after a criminal conviction, and the money goes into the general fund. Source: Institute for Justice (opens in a new tab)
  • Nebraska required a conviction for most forfeitures in 2016, by a 38 to 8 vote. In 2021, Maine became the fourth state to end civil forfeiture, after New Mexico, Nebraska and North Carolina. Sources: Institute for Justice (opens in a new tab), Institute for Justice (opens in a new tab)
  • Pennsylvania passed the first Clean Slate law in 2018, which seals old records automatically. By 2026, 14 states and D.C. had passed one, putting more than 18 million people on a path to full or partial sealing. Sources: Clean Slate Initiative (opens in a new tab), Clean Slate Initiative (opens in a new tab)
  • In 2007, facing a forecast of 14,000 more inmates in five years, Texas spent $241 million on treatment and diversion instead of new prisons. Since then its prison population has fallen by more than 34,000 people and it has closed 16 prison facilities. The Council of State Governments Justice Center, which helped design the plan, reports more than $1.6 billion saved or avoided. Texas's crime rate in 2009 was its lowest since 1974, though the numbers don't prove the reform caused that. Sources: Wisconsin Legislative Council (opens in a new tab), CSG Justice Center (opens in a new tab), CSG Justice Center (opens in a new tab)
  • In December 2018, Congress passed the First Step Act 87 to 12 in the Senate and 358 to 36 in the House. It lets judges go below some mandatory minimums and expands job training and drug treatment in federal prison. Source: Roll Call (opens in a new tab)

What it looks like where you live

In your town

  • Your county prosecutor decides which cases get charged. Ask every candidate whether they'll charge simple cannabis possession, and vote on the answer.
  • Find out if your county runs a record-sealing clinic. If it doesn't, ask the clerk of courts or a legal aid group to start one.
  • Ask your sheriff and police chief, at a public meeting, how much property they took last year from people who were never convicted.

Your state party

  • Pick one bill, like a Clean Slate law or a conviction requirement for forfeiture, and make it your affiliate's signature issue for a whole session.
  • Testify at every committee hearing on it. Bring a person it happened to, not a white paper.
  • Thank every lawmaker who votes yes, by name and in public, whatever their party. The First Step Act passed with both parties.

You, this week

  • Volunteer at a record-sealing or expungement clinic. Legal aid groups need help with paperwork.
  • Sign the next ballot petition on cannabis or sentencing in your state.
  • Write down one story in five sentences: someone who lost a job or a car over a charge that went nowhere. Send it to your state representative.

Say it in 10 seconds: "Right now police can take your cash without charging you, and you have to sue to get it back. We require a conviction first. No conviction, no taking."

Built on the book's pattern: what the system does, why it hurts you, what you get when it's gone. More in messages that land.

Watch out for

Legal, but only for the well connected. Ending a ban by handing out a few expensive licenses swaps one wall for another. The book's warning is that removing rules without redesigning the system creates "new gatekeepers." Push for a market anyone can enter.
