Next, cut the licenses, permits and fees that make ordinary work hard. These wins are cheap, fast and popular, and they show people what less friction feels like.

Why it comes here

Most of the government people run into isn't a ban. It's a form. The book says freedom usually disappears "Not through force. Through friction." A permit that takes six months is a ban for anyone who can't wait six months.

These fixes cost the state almost nothing, pay off right away, and win votes from people who'd never call themselves libertarian. That's why they come second: they build trust fast, and trust is what pays for the bigger steps.

Proof it works

  • On April 10, 2019, Arizona became the first state to recognize out-of-state job licenses, so licensed newcomers can keep working without redoing Arizona's training. By 2025, 28 states had some form of it. Sources: Ballotpedia (opens in a new tab), Reason (opens in a new tab)
  • Wyoming's Food Freedom Act took effect in 2015. It frees homemade food sold directly to an informed buyer from state and local licenses, permits and inspection. Source: Wyoming Legislature (opens in a new tab)
  • Seven states now allow sales of most homemade foods, including perishable ones. None of them found a foodborne illness caused by food sold under those laws. Source: Institute for Justice (opens in a new tab)
  • Since 2019, Texas cities and counties can't ban kids' lemonade stands or charge a permit fee for them, on private property or in public parks. Illinois stopped health departments from regulating lemonade sales by kids under 16 in 2021. Sources: Texas Legislature (opens in a new tab), Reason (opens in a new tab)
  • Homebrewing beer became legal under federal law in 1979. States kept their own bans, and Alabama and Mississippi were the last to legalize it, in 2013. Source: American Homebrewers Association (opens in a new tab)
  • Federal regulators once controlled who could haul freight by truck and what they could charge. After trucking was decontrolled, ending with the Motor Carrier Act of 1980, truckload rates fell about 25% after inflation from 1977 to 1982, and licensed carriers topped 40,000 by 1990. Supporters ranged from Gerald Ford and Jimmy Carter to Ted Kennedy. Source: Econlib (opens in a new tab)

What it looks like where you live

In your town

  • Count the steps. Pick one thing, like opening a home bakery or adding a deck, and write down every form, fee and day it takes. The book says to measure friction in time, cost and steps.
  • Ask your council for a shot clock: if the city doesn't answer a permit request in 30 days, it's approved. The book's rule is "If the system cannot respond, the answer becomes yes."
  • Check your town's rules on kids' stands, home businesses and food trucks. Any permit you find is your first ordinance to repeal.

Your state party

  • If your state doesn't recognize out-of-state licenses yet, make it a bill. Arizona's 2019 law is the model.
  • Push a food freedom bill modeled on Wyoming's.
  • Ask for a sunrise review: before the state licenses a new job, it has to show what harm the license prevents.

You, this week

  • Ask three people who work with their hands which license or permit made their life harder. Write down their words. That's your message.
  • If you're licensed and moved states, send your story to your state representative.
  • Buy from a home baker or a kid's stand, and tell them why you're glad it's legal.

Say it in 10 seconds: "If another state says you're qualified, you can work here immediately."

That line is straight from the book, chapter 8.

Watch out for

A new form for the old form. A "streamlined" process with a new office and a new fee isn't less government. Measure it: fewer steps, less time and lower cost, or it didn't count.
