Step 5: Push power down

Move what's left out of Washington and into the state, the town and the neighborhood. The closer a decision is to you, the faster you can see it fail and fix it.

Why it comes here

A bad federal law can last for decades because there's nothing to compare it to and no easy way out. The book says what separates markets from politics is feedback speed, not perfection. Fifty states and thousands of towns are thousands of experiments, and people can move to the ones that work. Many of the wins on steps 1 to 3 started in one state before the rest caught up.

But local isn't automatically free. The book warns that "Local politics rewards opposition," which is how a neighborhood board can block every new house. So push power down and protect individual rights at every level. Sometimes the state should stop a town from banning something peaceful, the way Texas did for kids' lemonade stands.

"Not perfection— but feedback speed."

The Asymmetric Libertarian, chapter 3

Proof it works

  • In 1923, New York repealed its state Prohibition enforcement law, leaving enforcement to about 250 federal agents. Supporters of repeal said 13,000 indictments had produced only 18 convictions. Source: American Heritage (opens in a new tab)
  • National Prohibition ended a decade later, on December 5, 1933, when the 21st Amendment was ratified. States could still keep their own bans. Source: Britannica (opens in a new tab)
  • Colorado and Washington legalized cannabis for adults in 2012 while the federal ban stood. As of 2026, 24 states and D.C. allow it. Source: Pew Research Center (opens in a new tab)
  • Patients in 40 states had a state right-to-try law before Congress passed one in 2018. Source: Foundation for Economic Education (opens in a new tab)
  • Moving power down cuts both ways. Homebrewing became legal under federal law in 1979, but Alabama and Mississippi kept their bans until 2013. Source: American Homebrewers Association (opens in a new tab)
  • The Free State Project started in 2001 with a goal of getting 20,000 liberty-minded people to move to one small state. It announced 20,000 pledges to move to New Hampshire on February 3, 2016. About 2,000 members had already moved by then. Sources: Free State Project (opens in a new tab), Fox News (opens in a new tab)

What it looks like where you live

In your town

  • Run for the seats that decide things close to home: township trustee, school board, zoning board, soil and water. Many have nobody running.
  • Show up when a neighbor wants to build. Zoning boards hear from the people who say no. Make them hear from the people who say yes.
  • Learn which decisions your town can make on its own and which ones the state took away. Each one is a fight you can pick.

Your state party

  • Find one federal ban your state spends its own police time enforcing, and push your state to stop, the way New York did with Prohibition in 1923.
  • Write a rights floor: a state law that stops towns from banning peaceful things, like Texas did for lemonade stands.
  • Build county and township groups, not just a state committee. Power you push down needs people there to use it.

You, this week

  • Write down who represents you at every level: township or city, county, school board and state.
  • Go to one local meeting where a decision gets made, not a party meeting.
  • If you're moving anyway, look at which states have done the most on steps 1 to 4.

Say it in 10 seconds: "Washington can't see your street. Your town can. Make decisions where people can see them and fix them."

Built on the book's pattern: what the system does, why it hurts you, what you get when it's gone. More in messages that land.

Watch out for

Trading a big boss for a small one. A county that bans what the state allows isn't progress. Push power down and protect individual rights at every level, or you just move the problem closer to home.
