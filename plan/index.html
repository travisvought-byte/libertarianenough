The plan

The plan: from more state to less, one step at a time

Nobody gets to push a button and end the state. So here's the road instead: six steps, in order, each one already done somewhere in America. Walk it with us whether you want a small government or none.

The 60-second version

"No government" isn't a plan. It's a destination. A plan says what you do first, what you do next, and how you keep what you win.

Start with the harm people feel today, like jail for a plant or police keeping cash from people never charged. Then clear the permission slips that make normal life hard. Then give people a way out of the systems that fail them. Lock in every win with spending caps and expiration dates. Push what's left down to the state, the town and the neighborhood. Last, hand it back to people who'll do it voluntarily. Each step makes the next one easier, because people trust what they can see.

The road, step by step

  1. 1

    Step 1 of 6

    Stop the harm

    End punishments for peaceful choices: jail for a plant, cops taking cash without a conviction, old records that follow people forever.

    Starts with: Cannabis, civil forfeiture, clean records, sentencing

  2. 2

    Step 2 of 6

    Tear up the permission slips

    Cut the licenses, permits and fees that stand between people and a job, a side business or a lemonade stand.

    Starts with: Job licenses, home food sales, permits, kids' stands

  3. 3

    Step 3 of 6

    Open the exits

    Let people leave systems that fail them before you shut anything down: school choice, the right to try, real competition.

    Starts with: School choice, right to try, open markets

  4. 4

    Step 4 of 6

    Cap it and set a clock

    Lock in each win so government can't quietly grow back: spending limits, and laws that end unless lawmakers renew them.

    Starts with: Spending caps, sunset reviews

  5. 5

    Step 5 of 6

    Push power down

    Move what's left from Washington to the state, the town and the neighborhood, where people can see it and fix it.

    Starts with: Federal to state to local, with rights protected at every level

  6. 6

    Step 6 of 6

    Hand it back

    Let people run services they already run well on their own, and test every handoff before the state lets go.

    Starts with: Volunteers, mutual aid, contract cities

Where the road ends

Libertarians split on the last mile. That's fine, because the first five steps are the same either way.

A small state

Government shrinks to courts, police and defense, and stops there. In his 1974 book Anarchy, State, and Utopia, philosopher Robert Nozick argued for this kind of minimal state: a "night watchman" that protects people against violence, theft and fraud, and does nothing else. Most minarchists and classical liberals stop here.

Source: Britannica (opens in a new tab)

No state

Anarcho-capitalists and agorists keep going until courts, security and everything else run on voluntary deals. It's the least tested stretch of road, which is one more reason to walk the tested part first and learn on the way.

More on the factions

What both ends share

Either way, the place we're heading has the same shape. Nobody, not a king, a mayor or a majority, gets to use force on peaceful people, and the rules are the same for everyone. That's the balance point: equal rights, nobody picking winners, and mistakes that teach instead of ruin. The book puts that last part as "Consequences that teach—not consequences that end participation."

Why this order

  1. Start where people are stuck. The book's first question is "Where are voters technically free—but practically stuck? That's where you start." Steps 1 and 2 are where people are most stuck.
  2. Win what people can feel. "Voters trust what they can see." Someone whose record got sealed, or whose license now works across the state line, believes the next step will work too.
  3. Build the exit before you close the door. The book says libertarianism "removes constraints— but fails to redesign the system that remains." Taking away a program with nothing in its place feels like loss, "And voters reject loss." So exits come at step 3 and handoffs wait until step 6.
  4. Lock in every win. Rules creep back. Caps and sunsets at step 4 keep the road from washing out behind you.
  5. Move decisions closer to people. Small units find and fix mistakes faster, as long as individual rights hold at every level.
  6. Alignment over purity. You don't need everyone to agree on the end of the road to walk the next mile together. "Agreement is total. Alignment is partial—and enough to win."

Whose order is this? The Asymmetric Libertarian is a campaign manual. It gives the method: start where people are stuck, win what they can see, make failure survivable, and build alignment over purity. It doesn't set an order for reforms. The order on this page is ours, built on that method. If you think step 4 should come first, make the case. Tell us, and we'll change it if you're right.

Where to start this week

  • If you have an hour: pick the step that makes you angriest and read its page. Each one ends with three things to do this week.
  • If you run a state party or county group: pick one bill from step 1 or 2, where support is widest, and make it the thing your group is known for. The organizing playbook shows how.
  • If you're running for office: use the 10-second message on each step page. Voters ask how long, how much and what happens if they mess up, and every step answers one of those. More in messages that land.
