Models we built

We build websites that people use every week, and those sites are how the causes behind them raise money. Here's what we've learned, so your state can copy it.

Model 1: build something useful, and the audience follows

Find Bingo (opens in a new tab) and Ohio Bingo (opens in a new tab) list bingo games so players can find one tonight. The Reefer (opens in a new tab) and OhioWeed.org (opens in a new tab) answer what cannabis law allows, state by state. None of them ask for anything up front. They're useful, so people come back, and an audience that trusts you is the start of every donor list.

Copy it: pick a question people in your state ask every week and nobody answers well. Where can I find a gun range? Which licenses does my trade need? What did my county commission vote on? Build the answer. If you need a whole site for it, we'll build it free.

Model 2: free for everyone, with a way to give back

The bingo sites run on a simple deal: listings are free for every hall, halls that want extra services can pay for them, and halls that want to can give back a share of their net proceeds. Nobody's locked out for not paying.

Copy it: give the service away, offer paid extras to the people who want them, and make giving back to the cause easy and optional.

Model 3: one goal, one deadline, one page

The campaigns that work have one dollar goal, one date and one page, and every site you run links to that page from its footer until the date passes.

Copy it: pick one number and one date, like a filing deadline or the end of a finance quarter, build one page on your own processor, and link it from everything you run.

Bingo and raffles: partner with a charity

Charity gaming is usually licensed to charities, not parties, so the move is to partner with one.

  • Ohio: a charitable organization applies to the Attorney General each year before January 1 for a bingo licence. It must show two years' continuous existence as a charitable organization in Ohio and an IRS determination letter under section 501(c)(3), (4), (7), (8), (10) or (19). Source: codes.ohio.gov (opens in a new tab)
  • New York: a State Board of Elections enforcement determination, about a county Republican committee's 2021 raffle, states that political parties and other political committees are specifically not authorized organizations for games of chance, including raffles, under General Municipal Law section 186. Source: electionenforcement.ny.gov (opens in a new tab)
  • New Mexico: in 2019 the Gaming Control Board told the Los Alamos Republican Party to cancel a raffle planned for its fall dinner and refund tickets. The board said political organizations don't meet the definition of a qualified organization under the Bingo and Raffle Act. Source: nmpoliticalreport.com (opens in a new tab)
  • Illinois is an exception: political committees may run raffles with a licence from the State Board of Elections. Only bona fide members may run the raffle, without pay, records must be kept for three years and reported, and all net proceeds go to the committee's lawful purposes. Source: elections.il.gov (opens in a new tab)
