The rules

The fastest way to kill a small party is a campaign-finance mess. The rules aren't hard, but they're real, and the first one is that you need a treasurer before you raise a dollar.

The basics

  • Federal registration, state or national party committee: register with the FEC within 10 days of raising more than $1,000 in contributions, or spending more than $1,000 on contributions or other expenditures, in a calendar year. Registration is a Statement of Organization (Form 1). Source: FEC (opens in a new tab)
  • Federal registration, local party organization: register if it raises more than $5,000 in contributions to influence a federal election, spends more than $1,000 on federal contributions or expenditures, or spends more than $5,000 on exempt party activities. Once registered, it is presumed affiliated with the state party and shares its contribution limits. Source: FEC (opens in a new tab)
  • Party organizations must pay for all activity in connection with federal elections with money that meets federal contribution limits and prohibitions. Source: FEC (opens in a new tab)
  • Federal rule 11 CFR 102.7: every political committee must have a treasurer, and it may not accept contributions or make expenditures while the treasurer's post is vacant, unless a designated assistant treasurer steps in. Source: Cornell LII (opens in a new tab)
  • State races follow state law, and states differ widely. As of February 2023, 11 states set no limits on individual donors, 19 put no limit on state party contributions to candidates, 8 apply individual limits to parties, and 19 set separate party limits. Source: NCSL (opens in a new tab)
  • A state-registered committee or unregistered local party that gives to federal candidates must use federally permissible funds, and may have to register with the FEC once its federal giving passes the thresholds. Source: FEC (opens in a new tab)

The checklist

  1. Name a treasurer and an assistant treasurer.
  2. Open a bank account in the committee's name. Never mix it with anyone's personal money.
  3. Find your state's campaign-finance office (usually the secretary of state or an ethics or elections commission) and read its guide for party committees.
  4. Track every dollar in and out, with names and addresses for donors, from day one.
  5. Calendar every filing deadline for the year in January.
  6. If you'll touch federal races, read the FEC's party guide before you spend a cent on them.
