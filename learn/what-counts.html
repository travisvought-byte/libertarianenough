What counts, and what doesn't

Libertarian is one of the most borrowed labels in politics. Here's the line, and the people standing on the wrong side of it.

The short definition

Libertarianism says each person owns their own life, and that starting force or fraud against peaceful people is wrong, whether a mugger does it or a government does. Libertarians want government shrunk to protecting rights (police, courts, defense) or, for the anarchist wing, gone. That one idea covers your wallet and your bedroom. If you only want one of the two freed, you're half way there.

Counts

  • Minarchists. Small state, only for protecting rights. The original Libertarian Party majority. More.
  • Anarcho-capitalists. No state at all, markets for everything including courts. Fully counts, roads question included. More.
  • Classical liberals. Free markets, free speech, rule of law, a modest state. The grandparents of the whole movement. More.
  • Left-libertarians, agorists and voluntaryists. Anti-state with a different list of villains, or a different strategy. Counts. More.
  • Bleeding-heart libertarians. Free markets justified partly by how they help the poor. Counts, and they'll send you a paper about it. More.
  • Paleolibertarians. Counts on policy. Their strategy fights are on the controversies page, and the "liberty for my people only" corner of it doesn't count at all (see below). More.
  • Objectivists. Adjacent. Ayn Rand recruited half the movement and rejected the label anyway. The feud.

Half credit

  • Wallet libertarians. Hate taxes, love the drug war. That's a conservative who likes the word "liberty."
  • Bedroom libertarians. Love legal weed, want a license for everything else. That's a progressive with a dispensary card.
  • "Fiscally conservative, socially liberal." Counts if you mean it all the way through: guns, licensing, school choice, sex work, all of it. Usually people mean "I don't want to talk about politics at work."

Doesn't count, and why

  • "Liberty for my people only." Rights belong to individuals, every one of them. Anyone who wants liberty for their own group and checkpoints for everyone else is a collectivist in a Gadsden flag. Even the Libertarian Party's current platform says it defends "the rights of every person, regardless of their race, ethnicity, or any other aspect of their identity" (plank 3.5, LP platform (opens in a new tab)).
  • Wanting a king, even a privately owned one. Swapping an elected government for an owner who can't be voted out is not less state. It's more state with a crown on it.
  • Sovereign citizens. A legal theory, not a philosophy, and courts reject it every time. Declaring your car a "private conveyance" won't get you out of a traffic ticket. It will get you a longer hearing.
  • Crony capitalism. Pro-market is not pro-business. A company lobbying for a license rule that keeps competitors out is using the state as a weapon. Libertarians are against that, even when the company sponsors the event.
  • Using the state on your enemies. If your plan needs government to punish people you don't like, it isn't a libertarian plan, however many Mises quotes are in the thread.
  • "Free speech" as a shield for being a jerk. Free speech means the government can't jail you for what you say. It doesn't mean nobody gets to call you a jerk, block you or decline to book you.

The stricter test

The Asymmetric Libertarian adds one more: "If it does not work in reality, it does not count." A position you can defend in a comment section but can't explain to a voter in ten seconds isn't a policy yet. It's a hobby.
