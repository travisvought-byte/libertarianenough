The voices

Who to read, who to watch, and who keeps getting quoted at meetings.

Listing someone here means they matter to the movement, not that we agree with them or that they agree with each other. Several of them would rather not share a page.

Thinkers

John Locke

1632–1704

English philosopher whose Two Treatises of Government (1689) grounded government in natural rights and consent; a founding text of liberalism.

Sources: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Adam Smith

1723–1790

Scottish economist and moral philosopher; The Wealth of Nations (1776) is often called the first comprehensive defense of free markets.

Sources: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab), Wikiwand (opens in a new tab)

Thomas Paine

1737–1809

Pamphleteer whose Common Sense (January 1776) made the popular case for American independence.

Sources: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Thomas Jefferson

1743–1826

Drafted the Declaration of Independence in 1776; his ideas on limited government shaped American political thought.

Source: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab)

Frédéric Bastiat

1801–1850

French economist and legislator; The Law (1850) argues law should protect persons and property and warns against 'legal plunder.'

Sources: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

John Stuart Mill

1806–1873

English philosopher; On Liberty (1859) defends individual freedom on utilitarian grounds.

Source: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab)

Lysander Spooner

1808–1887

Abolitionist and individualist anarchist; challenged the postal monopoly in 1844 and wrote No Treason: The Constitution of No Authority (1870).

Sources: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab), Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab)

Henry David Thoreau

1817–1862

Jailed in 1846 for refusing his poll tax over slavery and the Mexican War; his 1849 essay became known as 'Civil Disobedience.'

Source: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab)

Benjamin R. Tucker

1854–1939

Leading American individualist anarchist; edited the journal Liberty from 1881 to 1908.

Sources: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab), Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab)

Ludwig von Mises

1881–1973

Austrian School economist; author of Socialism (1922) and Human Action (1949); the Mises Institute is named for him.

Sources: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Henry Hazlitt

1894–1993

Journalist and economist; wrote Economics in One Lesson (1946) and helped found the Foundation for Economic Education.

Sources: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Leonard E. Read

1898–1983

Founded the Foundation for Economic Education in 1946 and led it until his death.

Sources: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

F. A. Hayek

1899–1992

Austrian-British economist; wrote The Road to Serfdom (1944), founded the Mont Pelerin Society (1947) and shared the 1974 Nobel Prize in economics.

Sources: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Ayn Rand

1905–1982

Novelist and philosopher who developed Objectivism; Atlas Shrugged (1957) drew many readers to individualism.

Sources: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Milton Friedman

1912–2006

Chicago economist; Capitalism and Freedom (1962), the 1976 Nobel Prize, and Free to Choose (1980); proposed school vouchers and argued for a volunteer military.

Source: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab)

Karl Hess

1923–1994

Former Goldwater speechwriter who moved toward anarchism; his essay 'The Death of Politics' (1969) became a rallying point for libertarians.

Sources: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Tonie Nathan

1923–2014

1972 Libertarian vice-presidential nominee and the first woman to receive an electoral vote.

Sources: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Murray Rothbard

1926–1995

Economist and leading theorist of anarcho-capitalism; wrote For a New Liberty (1973), helped found Cato, and later argued for a 'paleo' populist strategy.

Sources: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Ed Clark

1930–2025

1980 Libertarian presidential nominee whose 921,128 votes stood as the party's record for more than three decades.

Sources: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab), Reason (opens in a new tab)

Robert Nozick

1938–2002

Philosopher whose Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974) defended a minimal state and won the 1975 National Book Award.

Sources: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

David Nolan

1943–2010

Co-founder and first national chair of the Libertarian Party; created the Nolan Chart (1971).

Sources: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Institutions

Libertarian Party (opens in a new tab)

U.S. political party founded December 11, 1971; it has run a presidential candidate in every election since 1972.

Sources: Libertarian Party (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Mises Caucus

Faction within the Libertarian Party founded in 2017 by Michael Heise; won control of the national committee at the 2022 convention.

Sources: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab), Reason (opens in a new tab)

Reason (opens in a new tab)

Libertarian magazine and website ('Free Minds and Free Markets') founded in 1968, with a video arm, Reason TV.

Sources: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab), Reason (opens in a new tab)

Reason Foundation (opens in a new tab)

Policy research nonprofit founded in 1978 that publishes Reason.

Sources: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Cato Institute (opens in a new tab)

Washington, D.C., public-policy research organization founded in 1977 by Ed Crane with Charles Koch and Murray Rothbard; mission centers on individual liberty, limited government, free markets and peace.

Sources: Ballotpedia (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Mises Institute (opens in a new tab)

Educational, research and publishing institute for Austrian economics, founded in 1982 by Lew Rockwell in Auburn, Alabama.

Source: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Foundation for Economic Education (FEE) (opens in a new tab)

Founded in 1946 by Leonard Read with Henry Hazlitt; often called the oldest free-market think tank in the U.S.; now based in Atlanta.

Sources: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab), Foundation for Economic Education (opens in a new tab)

Institute for Justice (opens in a new tab)

Libertarian public-interest law firm founded in 1991 by Chip Mellor and Clint Bolick; litigates economic liberty, property rights, school choice and free speech cases.

Sources: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab), Institute for Justice (opens in a new tab)

Pacific Legal Foundation (opens in a new tab)

Public-interest law firm founded in Sacramento in 1973, described as the oldest libertarian public-interest law firm; focuses on property rights, equality and opportunity, and separation of powers.

Sources: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab), Ballotpedia (opens in a new tab)

Goldwater Institute (opens in a new tab)

Phoenix think tank founded in 1988 with Sen. Barry Goldwater's support; added a constitutional litigation arm in 2007.

Sources: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab), Ballotpedia (opens in a new tab)

Students For Liberty (opens in a new tab)

Student organization formed in 2008 after a conference at Columbia University; supports libertarian student groups.

Source: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Young Americans for Liberty (opens in a new tab)

Campus organization founded in 2008 out of the Students for Ron Paul effort.

Sources: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab), Foundation for Economic Education (opens in a new tab)

Institute for Humane Studies (opens in a new tab)

Founded in 1961 by economist F. A. 'Baldy' Harper to support classical liberal scholarship; associated with George Mason University since 1985.

Sources: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab), Ballotpedia (opens in a new tab)

Atlas Network (opens in a new tab)

Founded in 1981 by Antony Fisher; links several hundred free-market think tanks in more than 100 countries.

Sources: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Mont Pelerin Society

International society of classical liberal scholars founded by Hayek in 1947 near Lake Geneva.

Sources: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab), hoover.org (opens in a new tab)

Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab)

Online library with an encyclopedia of libertarian thinkers and ideas, essays and podcasts.

Source: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab)

Free State Project (opens in a new tab)

Effort launched in 2001 to move 20,000 liberty-minded people to New Hampshire (chosen in 2003); it passed 20,000 pledges in 2016.

Sources: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab), Free State Project (opens in a new tab)

Advocates for Self-Government (opens in a new tab)

Distributes the 'World's Smallest Political Quiz,' a version of the Nolan Chart.

Sources: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab), theadvocates.org (opens in a new tab)

Officeholders

Elected people who call themselves libertarian or libertarian-leaning, or who hold the libertarian line on enough votes that the movement claims them. Party labels are theirs.

Rand Paul

Republican In office

U.S. Senator, Kentucky (since 2011)

Describes himself as a constitutional conservative and has called himself libertarian-leaning; son of Ron Paul.

Sources: Wikiwand (opens in a new tab), Britannica (opens in a new tab), CBS News (opens in a new tab)

Thomas Massie

Republican In office

U.S. Representative, Kentucky's 4th District (since 2012)

Described by Reason and others as a libertarian Republican; lost his May 19, 2026 primary to Trump-backed Ed Gallrein, so his term ends in January 2027.

Sources: Reason (opens in a new tab), PBS (opens in a new tab), aljazeera.com (opens in a new tab)

Mike Lee

Republican In office

U.S. Senator, Utah (since 2011)

Described by the Associated Press as a libertarian-leaning conservative.

Sources: kutv.com (opens in a new tab), Ballotpedia (opens in a new tab)

Jared Polis

Democratic In office

Governor of Colorado

Was the only Democratic member of the House Liberty Caucus; Reason has called him perhaps the most libertarian governor in America, a label other libertarians dispute.

Sources: Wikiwand (opens in a new tab), Reason (opens in a new tab), coloradopolitics.com (opens in a new tab)

Justin Amash

Libertarian Former

Former U.S. Representative, Michigan's 3rd District (2011–2021)

Describes himself as a libertarian; joined the LP in April 2020 as the first Libertarian in Congress, ran in the 2024 Republican U.S. Senate primary, and returned to the LP in 2025.

Source: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Javier Milei

La Libertad Avanza In office

President of Argentina (since December 10, 2023)

Economist widely described as a libertarian; won the November 2023 runoff with 55.7%.

Sources: europarl.europa.eu (opens in a new tab), dawn.com (opens in a new tab), michiganpublic.org (opens in a new tab)

Ron Paul

Republican (1988 Libertarian nominee) Former

Former U.S. Representative, Texas

Libertarian presidential nominee in 1988 and Republican presidential candidate in 2008 and 2012.

Sources: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab), Wikiwand (opens in a new tab), Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Gary Johnson

Republican as governor; Libertarian nominee Former

Former Governor of New Mexico

Libertarian presidential nominee in 2012 and 2016; won 3.28% in 2016, the party's best result.

Sources: Ballotpedia (opens in a new tab), kolotv.com (opens in a new tab)

Marshall Burt

Libertarian Former

Former Wyoming state representative, House District 39 (elected 2020)

Won his seat in 2020 as a Libertarian, the party's first state legislative win in about two decades; lost re-election in 2022.

Sources: Libertarian Party (opens in a new tab), Reason (opens in a new tab)

Shows, podcasts and books

Reason TV (opens in a new tab)

video

Reason's video arm, publishing documentaries and commentary, also on YouTube.

Source: Reason (opens in a new tab)

EconTalk (opens in a new tab)

podcast

Weekly economics interview podcast hosted by Russ Roberts since 2006, from Liberty Fund's Library of Economics and Liberty.

Source: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Free to Choose (opens in a new tab)

TV series and book

Milton and Rose Friedman's 1980 PBS series and companion best-seller on markets and freedom.

Sources: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab), dallasfed.org (opens in a new tab)

Stossel TV (opens in a new tab)

video

Online channel journalist John Stossel launched in 2019 after careers at ABC News and Fox Business.

Source: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Part of the Problem (opens in a new tab)

podcast

Libertarian podcast hosted by comedian Dave Smith, a member of the LP's Mises Caucus.

Source: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Free Thoughts

podcast

Libertarianism.org's podcast on libertarian ideas and history.

Source: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab)

The Road to Serfdom (F. A. Hayek, 1944) (opens in a new tab)

book

Hayek's warning against central economic planning; a Reader's Digest condensation reached a mass audience in 1945.

Source: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Economics in One Lesson (Henry Hazlitt, 1946) (opens in a new tab)

book

Widely read short primer on free-market economics.

Source: penguinrandomhouse.com (opens in a new tab)

Capitalism and Freedom (Milton Friedman, 1962)

book

Case for free trade, school vouchers and limited government from a Nobel economist.

Source: Libertarianism.org (opens in a new tab)

For a New Liberty (Murray Rothbard, 1973) (opens in a new tab)

book

Often called the first modern free-market anarchist manifesto; free from the Mises Institute.

Source: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

The Machinery of Freedom (David Friedman, 1973)

book

Consequentialist case that law and defense could be supplied privately.

Source: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

Anarchy, State, and Utopia (Robert Nozick, 1974)

book

Philosophical defense of the minimal state; won the National Book Award.

Source: Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)
