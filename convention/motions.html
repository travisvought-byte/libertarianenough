Make a motion, appeal the chair, and know what outranks what

You don't need to memorize Robert's Rules. You need about a dozen sentences, the order motions outrank each other, and the nerve to walk to a microphone.

How a motion moves

  1. 1

    Get the floor

    Go to a floor microphone and wait to be recognized. Microphones are marked pro, con, or other. Start with your name and state: "Chair, Jane Smith, delegate from Ohio." Only urgent calls, like "Point of order!" or "Division!", come from your seat. Source: LPedia (opens in a new tab)

  2. 2

    Make the motion

    "I move that…" Say exactly what you want done, in one sentence. The chair can require it in writing, so have it written. Source: Libertarian Party (opens in a new tab)

  3. 3

    Someone seconds it

    A second only means one other delegate wants it discussed. No second, no motion. Line up your second before you walk to the mic.

  4. 4

    The chair states it

    Once the chair repeats it, it belongs to the convention, not you. You need permission to withdraw it.

  5. 5

    Debate, on a clock

    Speakers alternate for and against. Bylaws proposals get 2 minutes of explanation and 8 of debate. Platform proposals get 2 and 15. Source: Libertarian Party (opens in a new tab)

  6. 6

    The vote

    The chair takes a voice vote. Doubt it? "Division!" gets a standing vote, and if 20 delegates object to the chair's call on that, it's counted. Sources: Libertarian Party (opens in a new tab), LPedia (opens in a new tab)

Steps follow Robert's Rules, 12th edition, as summarized in Jim Slaughter's chart of motions (opens in a new tab).

What to say

Pick what you want to happen. Say the line. The note under it is what happens next.

Bring up something new

Chair, Jane Smith, delegate from Ohio. I move that…

Needs a second. Majority, except bylaws, new planks and resolutions, which take 2/3.

Change the wording

I move to amend by striking “…” and inserting “…”.

Needs a second. Majority, even when the motion itself needs 2/3. Two levels of amendment, max.

End debate and vote now

I move the previous question.

Needs a second. No debate. Takes 2/3. Shouting “Question!” from your seat does nothing.

The chair broke a rule

Point of order!

You can say it from your seat and interrupt. No second. The chair rules. Raise it when it happens or it's too late, unless it breaks the bylaws.

You think the chair ruled wrong

I appeal from the decision of the chair.

Right after the ruling. Needs a second. A majority against the chair overturns it. A tie keeps the ruling.

You doubt a voice vote

Division!

From your seat. Forces a standing vote. If 20 delegates object to the chair's call on that, it gets counted.

You don't know how to do something

Parliamentary inquiry: how would I…?

The chair answers. No vote. This is the one nobody uses and everybody should.

You need a fact

Request for information: does this amendment change…?

It has to be a question. If a speaker yields to answer, it comes out of their time.

Take something out of order

I move to suspend the rules and take up…

Needs a second. No debate. Takes 2/3. Can't suspend the bylaws. Usually fails.

Kill it without a straight vote

I move to postpone indefinitely.

Needs a second. Debatable, including the main motion's merits. Majority.

Redo a vote

I move to reconsider the vote on…

Only if you voted on the winning side, the same day or the next business day. Majority.

Get back on schedule

I call for the orders of the day.

One person can demand it. Setting the schedule aside takes 2/3.

Sources: AFT (opens in a new tab), Libertarian Party (opens in a new tab), LPedia (opens in a new tab), Robert's Rules Association (opens in a new tab)

What outranks what

While a motion is on the floor, you can only make a motion that ranks higher. That's precedence. If someone moves to amend, you can move to end debate on it, but you can't bring up new business. Votes go from the top down: the highest-ranking pending motion gets decided first.

Point of order, appeal, inquiries, division and suspending the rules sit outside the ladder. They come up whenever they're needed and get handled right away.

Appealing the chair, step by step

  1. Wait for a ruling. You can only appeal a decision the chair actually made. If they haven't ruled, raise a point of order first and make them rule.
  2. Appeal immediately. "I appeal from the decision of the chair." If other business starts, you've lost it.
  3. Get a second. Have one ready.
  4. The chair explains first and last. Usually the appeal is debatable. It isn't when it's about decorum, the order of business, or a question that can't be debated.
  5. The question is "Shall the decision of the chair stand?" A majority voting no overturns it. A tie keeps it.
  6. Expect to lose. In 2020 delegates appealed at least nine times and won once, 550 to 353. Save it for a ruling that's clearly wrong, and talk to the parliamentarian at the head table first. Source: Libertarian Party (opens in a new tab)

Source: AFT (opens in a new tab)

When the chair counts wrong

The party's own manual warns that chairs sometimes apply thresholds to everyone present instead of those present and voting. If a 2/3 vote is ruled failed and the yes votes were 2/3 of the votes actually cast, raise a point of order right then. Source: LPedia (opens in a new tab)
