Build from the precinct up

The Republican and Democratic parties aren't really national organizations. They're thousands of county committees stacked on top of each other, and each county committee is a list of precinct seats. Whoever fills those seats picks the leaders, writes the rules and, in some states, fills empty public offices. Here's how it works, using Ohio and two other states as examples.

How it's built

  • Ohio law sets up each major party's county central committee with one member from each election precinct (or, if the outgoing committee chooses, one per city ward and one per township). Members are elected by direct vote at the primary in even-numbered years and serve two- or four-year terms, as party rules set. Source: FindLaw (Ohio Revised Code) (opens in a new tab)
  • The same Ohio section creates a state central committee of two members, one man and one woman, from each congressional or state senate district, as the outgoing committee decides. Source: FindLaw (Ohio Revised Code) (opens in a new tab)
  • In Ohio, a minor party's controlling committees are chosen under the party's own rules, not by the primary ballot that major parties use. Source: FindLaw (Ohio Revised Code) (opens in a new tab)
  • In Multnomah County, Oregon, precinct committee persons appear on the even-year primary ballot, only voters of that party and precinct see the race, and a candidate needs just three votes to win. Candidates must have been registered with the party for 180 days before the filing deadline, write-in candidates can file, and the term is two years. Source: multco.us (opens in a new tab)
  • Washington Democrats' precinct committee officers file with the county elections office during filing week, with no filing fee. A sole filer is automatically elected, and an empty seat can be filled by appointment from the county or legislative district chair. Precinct officers help elect local, county and state party leaders and nominate people to fill state House and Senate vacancies. Source: wadems.org (opens in a new tab)
  • Why the seats matter: in Ohio, when a county office such as sheriff, auditor or commissioner falls vacant and its last holder was elected as a party nominee, that party's county central committee appoints the replacement, meeting 5 to 45 days after the vacancy. Source: codes.ohio.gov (opens in a new tab)

What a libertarian affiliate should do with this

  1. Map your seats. List every precinct in your county. That's your org chart. A name goes next to each one, even if it's "open."
  2. Recruit a precinct captain before a county chair. A county chair with no precinct captains is a person with a title.
  3. Know your state's minor-party rules. In Ohio, minor parties pick committees under their own rules, so you can build the structure now without waiting for a ballot.
  4. If you're a liberty Republican or Democrat, file for your precinct seat. In some places three votes wins it. Nobody will stop you, because nobody else filed.
