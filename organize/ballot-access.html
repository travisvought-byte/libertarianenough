Ballot access

A party that isn't on the ballot is a club. Ballot access rules are written by the parties already on the ballot, which tells you how friendly they are.

How it works

  • A party usually gets on the ballot in one of three ways, depending on the state: a candidate passes a vote threshold, enough voters register with the party, or the party files a petition. It keeps that status by passing a later vote test or keeping a minimum registration. Ohio's vote test is 3%. Source: Ballotpedia (opens in a new tab)
  • Petition requirements range widely: from 0.1% of registered voters in Hawaii to 10% of the last gubernatorial vote in California. Arkansas, Colorado, Maryland, Missouri and South Carolina each set a fixed 10,000 signatures, North Dakota 7,000, and many states use 1% to 3% of a recent gubernatorial vote. Source: Ballotpedia (opens in a new tab)
  • Ballot Access News, Richard Winger's newsletter, noted in 2014 that in 38 states one petition puts a new party's nominees on the ballot for every office. Illinois instead needs separate petitions, about 25,000 signatures for a statewide slate alone. Source: Ballot Access News (opens in a new tab)

What to do

  1. Know your state's rule cold. Vote test, registration count or petition, and what keeps you on.
  2. Plan petition season like a campaign. Count the signatures, add a 30% cushion for bad ones, and set weekly targets.
  3. Petition where people already are. Farmers markets, festivals, polling places on election day where allowed.
  4. Sign the petition, purists. Yes, even if you think voting legitimizes the state. Signing a petition so other people get a choice is about as voluntary as politics gets.
